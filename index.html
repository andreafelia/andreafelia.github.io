Case study 01

Sociolla beauty product database

  • MySQL
  • SQLite
  • Python
  • Database for Data Science
  • Jun 2026
  • Group of 5

Question

Which product categories and brands do Sociolla shoppers want most, rate best, and buy again?

The raw data was one flat CSV file of product listings. Before anyone could answer that question reliably, the data had to be cleaned and stored in a structure that could be queried.

What we found

Skincare dominates demand, and everyday essentials earn the most loyalty. Toner is the most wishlisted category (474,665 wishlists), while Hand Wash has the highest repurchase intent: 85.3% of respondents say they would buy again.

7,636products
321brands
19 → 29columns before and after parsing
27.5%of activation dates missing (2,102 rows)

My part. Part of the data pre-processing, the relational tables, and the SQL implementation: building the database, the CRUD operations, and the analytical queries shown below.

Team (Group A4). Genisha Almyra Mumtaz (ER diagram, dashboard, conclusions), Haikal Faiz (introduction, dashboard testing), Maria Ellora Damarkasih (relational tables, SQL implementation), Muhammad Varendra (ER diagram, dashboard).

Open the full analysisdata, method, evidence and limitations

The data

Source: a dataset of Sociolla product listings (products_all_brands.csv). Prices and ratings were stored as text dictionaries, categories were nested, and several rating aspects were empty for products where they do not apply, such as scent for a makeup brush.

Approach

  1. Clean. Parsed price and rating dictionaries into numeric columns. Missing activation dates were imputed with the median date, and rating gaps were filled with the median of the relevant product category only where the aspect applies.
  2. Model. Normalized the flat file into five related tables: Brand, Category, Product, Rating and Engagement, linked by primary and foreign keys.
  3. Load. Imported the cleaned CSV through a staging table, then populated the five tables in dependency order (ETL).
  4. Query. Wrote 13 analytical queries: 3 multi-table JOINs, 5 aggregations with GROUP BY and HAVING, and 5 rankings.
  5. Present. My teammates built a web dashboard on top of the database to run the queries and chart the results.

Evidence: one of the queries

Repurchase intent by category. NULLIF guards against division by zero, and HAVING drops categories with 100 or fewer respondents so a handful of reviews cannot top the ranking.

SELECT
    c.category_default,
    SUM(e.total_repurchase_yes_count)   AS repurchase_yes,
    SUM(e.total_repurchase_no_count)    AS repurchase_no,
    SUM(e.total_repurchase_maybe_count) AS repurchase_maybe,
    ROUND(SUM(e.total_repurchase_yes_count) * 100.0 / NULLIF(
        SUM(e.total_repurchase_yes_count + e.total_repurchase_no_count
        + e.total_repurchase_maybe_count), 0), 2) AS pct_repurchase_yes
FROM Category c
JOIN Product p    ON c.category_id = p.category_id
JOIN Engagement e ON p.product_id  = e.product_id
GROUP BY c.category_id, c.category_default
HAVING SUM(e.total_repurchase_yes_count + e.total_repurchase_no_count
    + e.total_repurchase_maybe_count) > 100
ORDER BY pct_repurchase_yes DESC
LIMIT 15;

  • The single most reviewed product is Garnier Micellar Water with 21,536 reviews.
  • COSRX holds three of the five most wishlisted products, led by Low pH Good Morning Cleanser (58,568 wishlists).
  • Premium products keep good ratings: SK-II Facial Treatment Essence costs about Rp3.05 million and is rated 4.45 from 910 reviews.

What a business could do with this

  • Prioritize stock and promotion for toner, serum and moisturizer, where wishlist demand is concentrated.
  • Treat everyday essentials such as hand wash and cotton pads as loyalty products, suited to bundles or subscriptions.

Limitations

  • Brand rankings by average rating favor small brands with few reviews. Rankings that set a minimum review count are more trustworthy.
  • Some products show a recommendation rate above 100% because a shopper can recommend a product more than once. That metric should not be read as a true percentage.
  • High-priced products with good ratings show a correlation, not proof that price causes quality.
  • The data is a snapshot of listings, not sales, so wishlists and reviews stand in for demand.
Case study 02

Electricity demand forecasting and volatility analysis

  • Python
  • statsmodels
  • arch
  • Forecasting Methods
  • Jun 2026
  • Group of 4

Question

How much electricity will be consumed each day over the next two weeks, and how uncertain is that number?

A grid operator needs both answers. The forecast sets the plan, and the uncertainty sets how much reserve capacity to keep ready.

What we found

Demand follows a strong weekly rhythm, and its uncertainty changes over time. Forecast weekday demand is about 6,250 to 6,630 MW against 5,550 to 5,940 MW on weekends. A volatility model on top of the forecast gives prediction intervals that widen and narrow with recent turbulence.

2,629daily observations, 2019 to Mar 2026
10variables (consumption, production, sources)
0missing values or duplicates
7 daysseasonal period

My part. I did the model diagnostics and the forecasting: residual plots, ACF and PACF, Ljung-Box, Shapiro-Wilk and ARCH-LM tests, then the 14-day mean and volatility forecasts and their combined intervals. I wrote those two chapters of the report.

Team (Group I). Inshovi Amiroh, Ardelia Citta Azizah, Rafi Aulia.

Open the full analysisdata, method, evidence and limitations

The data

Source: the public Kaggle dataset “Hourly Electricity Consumption and Production in Romania”, aggregated from hourly readings to daily averages.

Approach

  1. Explore. Checked structure, missing values, duplicates, outliers and correlations between energy sources.
  2. Test. The ADF test rejected a unit root (statistic −4.20, p = 0.0007), so no differencing was needed. ACF spikes at lags 7, 14 and 21 confirmed weekly seasonality.
  3. Model the mean. Compared five SARIMA candidates and chose SARIMA(1,0,1)(1,0,1)7: close to the best AIC and BIC with fewer parameters, all of them significant.
  4. Model the variance. An ARCH-LM test on the residuals was strongly significant (statistic 1,411.85, p < 0.001), so we fitted GARCH models to the residuals and selected GARCH(2,2) by AIC.
  5. Diagnose and forecast. Checked residuals, then produced 14-day forecasts with intervals that combine both models.

What an operator could do with this

  • Plan for weekday demand several hundred MW above weekend demand.
  • Size reserve capacity from the upper bound of the interval, which moves with recent volatility, instead of a fixed margin.

Limitations

  • The Ljung-Box test still rejects at lags 7, 14 and 21, so the SARIMA residuals keep some autocorrelation. The data has more than one seasonal cycle and the model captures only the weekly one.
  • In GARCH(2,2) only the first ARCH term is statistically significant. A simpler variance model would likely do as well.
  • Residuals are heavier-tailed than a Normal distribution, so the 95% intervals may be too narrow on extreme days.
  • The model uses past consumption only. Temperature and public holidays would likely improve it.
Case study 03

S&P 500 return simulation and random number generator evaluation

  • Python
  • NumPy
  • SciPy
  • Statistical Computing
  • Oct 2026
  • Group of 5

Question

If you simulate daily stock returns, how far can you trust the result?

Two things can go wrong: the random number generator underneath the simulation, and the statistical model on top of it. This project tests both against ten years of real index data.

What we found

The generator was fine. The model was the weak point. Mersenne Twister passed every randomness check, but real returns have far heavier tails than a Normal distribution (excess kurtosis 15.76), so a Normal simulation understates the chance of extreme days.

2,516daily log returns, Dec 2014 to Dec 2024
503five-day trading blocks
0.0113daily standard deviation
53.7%of days the index rose

Team. Moza Adisa, Suci Hajra, Reksa Utami, Najwa Syakila Ramadani.

Open the full analysisdata, method, evidence and limitations

The data

Source: S&P 500 index closing values from a public Kaggle dataset. No missing values or duplicate dates.

Approach

  1. Describe. Computed log returns and counted up-days in each five-day block.
  2. Simulate. Generated uniform random numbers with Mersenne Twister, turned them into Normal returns with the polar method, and into weekly up-day counts with the inverse-transform method for a Binomial(5, 0.537).
  3. Compare generators. Ran the same simulation with three linear congruential generators of different quality.
  4. Check reproducibility. Repeated runs with the same seed, then with 30 different seeds at three sample sizes.
  5. Test fit. Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests, chi-square tests and Q-Q plots for the generator, the simulated sample and the real data.

Evidence

CheckResult
Weak generator (LCG, modulus 16)Only 8 distinct values. Simulated mean −0.684 where 0 was expected, and a bigger sample did not fix it.
Mersenne Twister uniformityMean off by 0.000512 from theory, lag-1 correlation −0.004.
SpeedMersenne Twister ran about 36 times faster than a loop-based LCG.
Same seed, three runsIdentical output every time.
Up-days per week vs Binomial(5, 0.537)Consistent with the model (chi-square p = 0.058).

Why it matters

  • Risk estimates built on a Normal assumption will understate large losses. A heavier-tailed model is the safer choice for anything that depends on extremes.
  • Record the seed, the generator and the library version with every simulation so results can be reproduced.

Limitations

  • Returns were simulated as independent draws. Real returns show volatility clustering, which this model ignores.
  • One index over one decade. Results may differ for single stocks or other periods.

Two faces of nutrition in West Java

  • Python
  • scikit-learn
  • SciPy
  • Multivariate Analysis
  • Apr 2026
  • Individual

Question

Do people in West Java’s cities and regencies eat differently, and does poverty explain it?

This one is all mine, from the analysis to the infographic design. It is the project that best shows the two things I like doing together: statistics and visual communication.

What we found

The higher the poverty rate, the lower the quality of the diet. One principal component captures 90.9% of the variation in six nutrition indicators, and it lines poverty and grain dependence up against calories, protein and animal-source food.

27regencies and cities in West Java
6indicators of diet and poverty
18 vs 9regencies compared with cities
2023SUSENAS household survey year
Open the full analysisdata, method, evidence and limitations

The data

Sources: BPS West Java consumption patterns (SUSENAS, March 2023) and the National Food Agency’s per-capita protein figures.

Approach

  1. Reduce. PCA on standardized indicators. Only the first component passes the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue 5.66).
  2. Group. K-means with two clusters separates 17 lower-nutrition areas from 10 better-nourished ones (silhouette score 0.566).
  3. Test. Hotelling’s T² compares the mean nutrition profile of regencies and cities.
  4. Design. Turned the results into a single infographic for a general audience.

Evidence

CheckResult
Variance explained by PC190.9%, rising to 97.1% with PC2
Hotelling’s T², regencies vs citiesT² = 81.129 against a critical value of 19.492 (p = 0.000021)
Calories per person per day1,907 kcal in regencies, 2,027 kcal in cities
Gap between cities and regenciesAbout 120 kcal and 7.6 g of protein per person per day

Limitations

  • 27 areas is a small sample, and the two groups are unequal in size (18 and 9).
  • The link between poverty and diet is an association across areas. It does not show what happens inside any one household.
